The Product

Skrimp collapses the grocery paradox. Deals, meals, and a shopping list — in one workflow.

Canadian families already do this. Every week, in their heads: scan the flyer, price the recipe mentally, cross-reference two stores, carry the number home, discover at the aisle that the deal expired Thursday. The workflow exists. It just costs forty-five minutes and a lot of goodwill.

Skrimp doesn't ask families to change their behaviour. It automates the part that was always tedious — and closes the loop all the way to the cart.

The Context

Food inflation hit 40-year highs. A family of four in Canada now spends over $16,000 a year.

Up 28.6% since 2020. Younger households — already carrying 30% less generational wealth than boomers at the same age — feel this differently than the statistic suggests. It's not abstract inflation. It's the item you put back. The recipe you stopped making. The Sunday session that used to feel like cooking and now feels like damage control.

"People can't really control their car insurance or their rent or their utilities. But grocery bills felt like something we could actually tackle." — Henrietta van Niekerk, CTV News, Sept 2025
Every family is already running a manual savings algorithm in their head every week. Nobody asked them to. Nobody gave them a better option.
This isn't a motivation problem. It's a tooling problem. Give people the right system and they'll do the rest.
Research & Findings

We listened — not just surveyed. Interviews, listening sessions, community conversations.

The methodology was deliberate — listening sessions, not questionnaires. Not "what would you want from a grocery app?" but "tell me about last Sunday." Ask someone their grocery budget and they give you a number. Ask them to narrate the shop and they describe a system — intricate, habitual, entirely their own — they didn't know they had. Flyer-first thinking. Mental price matching. Brand loyalty that predates memory. Routes through the store that save six minutes. Recipes never written down because they live in the body. The GBDA training holds: qualitative first, quantitative to confirm. The insight wasn't that people want to save money. Everyone wants that. The insight was: people are already the system. They are already doing this work, alone, in their heads, every single week. Skrimp's job wasn't to design something new — it was to automate something ancient.

Constraints

A tight scope — and families in immediate need — shaped every decision.

📄 Flyer data is unstructured chaos

Weekly flyers arrive with no standard format, no consistent product taxonomy, and no guarantee last week's layout matches this one. The pipeline had to be designed for variation, not consistency.

🔤 Ingredients have too many names

"Chicken thighs," "bone-in thighs," "poultry" — same ingredient, entirely different strings. Resolving those aliases across recipes and flyers was the quiet core of the whole system.

👤 Feature creep would have killed us

Solo founder, real users, real need. Every build decision had to prove immediate value — or it didn't ship. Ruthlessness about scope was the only way to stay moving.

💸 Can't charge families who can't afford food

The user base is cost-stressed by definition. Free first isn't a growth hack — it's the only ethical starting point. Skrimp had to earn the relationship before it could ask for anything.

The Problem & Our Scope

Skrimp is not a meal kit. Not a subscription box. Not another app that pretends to care about your budget. It is connective tissue — unifying deals, meals, and shopping into a single workflow that feels obvious once you're in it, and inexplicable that it didn't exist before.

Ethical guardrails weren't added later. They were constraints from the first commit: minimum data collected, plain-language explanations for every pricing call, easy edit and delete flows, no dark patterns pushing upsells at users who are already financially stretched.

Recipes reviewed for cultural bias. Allergen filters that persist across weeks without being asked again. No "one perfect plan" — the goal is agency returned to the household, not dependence on an algorithm that doesn't know what Tuesday looks like in your kitchen.

Ideation

Every flow had to answer one question: does this reduce the user's cognitive load?

The philosophical flip came early and shaped everything that followed. Most food apps start with a recipe — beautiful, curated, aspirational — and then, quietly, price you out of it. Skrimp inverts that entirely. What's on sale at your store this week is the prompt. The meal plan is the answer. Deals aren't a filter applied after you've committed to a recipe. They're the foundation the plan is built on.

If a step felt like homework, it got cut. Journey maps were ruthless: postal code → store → constraints → plan → list. Anything that didn't earn its place in that chain got removed. The listening sessions held the standard throughout: users can rarely say what they want, but they can describe — with surprising precision — exactly what's breaking their current system. We built for that.

Budget-first, not recipe-first. The plan hits your number before you fall in love with it. Not the other way around.
Defaults do the work. Power users get the controls. Everyone else gets a plan that simply works.

Must-haves

The non-negotiables. Built-in, not bolted on.

🧭 Explainable savings

Every meal shows its working — not just a total, a reason. "Cheap" is a claim. A line item is evidence. Families under financial pressure have been misled enough. Skrimp doesn't ask for trust it hasn't earned.

🔁 Swap and recalculate

Change one ingredient. Every number updates instantly. Swaps surface substitutes; use-up suggestions prevent the half-bag of lentils from disappearing to the back of the cupboard. The plan is alive — not a document you print and abandon.

👨‍👩‍👧 Built for households, not individuals

One person plans. Everyone else arrives at the aisle with half the context. Household sharing and real-time sync exist because meal planning was never supposed to be a solo activity — and designing it that way was always leaving something essential out.

🛡️ Minimum viable data

Families under financial pressure have less margin for everything — including data risk. Skrimp collects what's needed for utility, explains why, and makes deletion a single action. This was a constraint before it was a principle.

Design Process

From listening sessions to a live product — and an ongoing conversation with users.

5.1 // Meal Engine & Data Pipelines

Flyers don't cooperate. They arrive as PDFs — inconsistent layouts, no shared taxonomy, no guarantee this week's format matches last week's. Every Monday is a negotiation with data that was never designed to be machine-readable. Building a reliable meal engine on top of that required treating chaos as a given, not an exception. Nothing reaches a family's grocery list on the algorithm's first pass. Products earn their place.

The second problem was quieter and harder. Ingredients don't have one name. A recipe calls for chicken. The flyer has "bone-in thighs." Are those the same for pricing? They are — but only if the system is built to know that. Resolving those relationships, quietly, across retailers and hundreds of recipes, is what makes the price calculations feel trustworthy rather than approximate. That work is not glamorous. It is the load-bearing wall.

Every plan shows its working — which items are on sale, which store, what they cost this week. Not a black box. A receipt you can interrogate.
Resilient by design — handles layout changes, missing products, and store quirks without surfacing the chaos to the user.

5.2 // Prototyping & Iteration

Prototyped mobile-first for in-aisle use, desktop for the Sunday planning session. But the most important thing testing surfaced wasn't a UI fix. It wasn't a screen at all.

Meal planning is not a solo activity. One person is almost always "the planner." Their partner shows up at the store with the list and none of the context — why this brand, why chicken this week, why no pasta. The planning is done. But it belongs to one person, in one head, invisible to everyone else who has to live by it. Household sharing and real-time sync weren't a feature for a later sprint. They were the thing the product was missing. Adding them didn't upgrade Skrimp. It finished it. The swap & recalc pattern, per-meal totals, allergen presets that persist week to week — all of it came from watching real households hit the same wall.

Per-meal and per-week totals update on every change. The budget number is always live — never a surprise at checkout.
Allergen filters, store preferences, and dietary flags persist. You set them once; the plan remembers.
No dark patterns. No upsell friction aimed at users who are already financially stretched. That was a design constraint, not an afterthought.

Growing a Team

Six engineering interns. One or two terms of university between them. Real production code.

Six engineers joined Skrimp over the course of building it — not all at once, in rotations, one or two at a time. All funded by UWaterloo through the Bridging Entrepreneurs to Students program. Most were in their first year of computer science. They could write code. What they hadn't yet learned was to think about systems.

That gap is smaller than it sounds and harder to close than it appears. First-year engineers aren't dangerous because they don't know enough. They're challenging because they don't know what they don't know yet. In a classroom, that's fine — mistakes are low stakes, isolated, graded. In a live startup, the wrong refactor doesn't fail the assignment. It compounds quietly for six weeks and then breaks something real.

"You're not teaching them to code. They already know how to code. You're teaching them to ask 'what does this break?' before they write the first line." — Henrietta van Niekerk

Systems thinking — holding the whole in mind while working on a single part — is learnable. But it requires someone willing to slow down in the middle of a deadline to explain why the shortcut that works today creates the emergency next month. That slowing down has a cost. You do it anyway.

The moments that stayed: the intern who wanted to delete a database field "just to clean things up." The one who learned, through a real incident, why staging environments aren't optional. The one who started asking "what could break?" before writing a line of code. That last one is the moment. That's the shift — from someone who can write code to someone who can build something.

There is no playbook for mentoring first-years on a live startup at 21 while finishing your own degree. The institutional knowledge lives mostly in conversation: why we do it this way, what happened last time we didn't, what constraint makes the obvious answer wrong. Writing that down is hard. Living it is harder. Watching someone else learn it, through their own version of the same mistakes, is something else entirely.

What I didn't expect to learn about leadership:

Your job isn't to fill the knowledge gap. It's to make it visible — to the intern, to themselves. The ones who leave as good engineers are the ones who learned to see their own blind spots.

What I'd do differently:

Onboard to the why before the how. An intern who understands the product's constraints will make better decisions than one who only knows the codebase. Context is the real onboarding.

What mentorship actually produces:

Not a feature list — though those mattered. The output is a person who leaves with a working model of how production systems fail, and the habit of caring about that before it happens.

Takeaways

What this taught me that a classroom couldn't.

"Families testing the app have reported cutting their grocery bills by as much as $100/week, close to $5,000/yr." — Waterloo Region Record, 2025

01

Automate what already exists

Families were already running the algorithm. In their heads, every week, for free, at cost to their sanity. Skrimp didn't invent a behaviour — it inherited one and made it run without the labour.

02

The last mile is the product

A plan that ends at a list is a plan that depends on the user to finish it. The Chrome extension exists because "we got you to the shopping list" was not good enough. The loop had to close.

03

Household beats personal

The planning being solo was never a user preference. It was a gap in the product. When we closed it, retention followed. The unit isn't the user. It's the household.

04

Trust is the product

Families under financial stress don't want to be impressed. They want to believe the number. Every decision — explainable savings, transparent swaps, no dark patterns — was an argument that the number on screen is one they can trust.

🛒 What I didn't expect

The thing that surprised me most wasn't the savings. It was the tone of voice.

Users didn't say "I saved $80 this week." They said things like: "I don't have to think about it anymore." "Sunday isn't a thing for us now." "My partner finally knows what we're having." That's not a savings story. That's a stress story. The problem was never purely financial — it was the invisible weight of managing money, alone, every week, in your head. Skrimp didn't solve a grocery problem. It solved a cognitive one. The savings are real, confirmed by families, confirmed by the press. But what people feel is the absence of something. The weekly drain that stopped.

That is what AI should do. Not impress you. Disappear.

What I can't say yet — and why that's okay

There is an entire category of work inside Skrimp I am proud of and not showing here. The architecture decisions that took months to arrive at. The commercial work still in motion. The technical choices that will shape this product for years and belong to the future, not a portfolio page written in the present tense.

That's the right call. And it is a strange thing — to have built something you can't fully show. The best work I've done on Skrimp isn't on this page. The most interesting problems I've solved are still inside a codebase I'm not opening up yet.

What you've read is the public surface: the design philosophy, the research, the leadership, the decisions I can speak to without compromising what's still being made. It is not a small surface. But it is a surface.

The discipline of keeping quiet about active development — of understanding that the story gets told when it's done, not while it's happening — is its own kind of skill. You don't perform progress. You make it, quietly, and you wait. Some things are worth waiting for.

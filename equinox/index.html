The game

You are not the hero. You are the person the hero hired. The wizard’s tower is meant to burn a light that ends winter and brings spring, and it has been dark long enough that the town below is frightened. They hire you, an adventurer at the tail end of a bad run, to go up there and find out why.

Four floors. Nine artefacts. No fail states, no timer, no combat, nothing at all to lose. You clean centuries of neglect off things, put fractured things back together, and read the wizard’s notes as the journal hands them to you, until it becomes clear what he did. At the top there is a beacon with three concentric rings and nine seasonal inscriptions, and it is not going to hint at the answer.

Fourteen people built it in eighteen days. Four of us wrote code, and 281 commits later it was on itch.

Where I Came In

It was my first game jam, and the first time I had used Godot for anything real.

Another programmer on the team built the player movement and stood the 3D pipeline up inside the first forty eight hours, which is the only reason a beginner was useful by day three.

Getting the substrate solid in the first two days is the highest leverage thing anybody can do on a jam team, and I have thought about it a lot since, because I now do a version of that job on a much larger volunteer project.

What I got instead Every moment where the game answers the player. The transition when you finish a floor. The artefact turning clean. The minigame closing itself. The journal page unlocking. That is one job, and it turned out to be the job the scoreboard was measuring.
The Limitation

Winter was the theme. “Overcomplicated Screen Transitions” was the rule.

It is the one category every entry had to compete in, and we came second in it on the way to winning the jam outright.

The jam carried one required limitation: every scene, level or room change had to be an elaborate transition. Most teams read that as a visual dare, so make a nicer wipe. We read it as a brief about pacing. In a game with no fail states and no timer, transitions are the only punctuation you have. They are the difference between four rooms and four chapters.

I built the floor transitions. They live in portal.gd and the transition canvas.

The Minigames

Three of the four floors end the same way, and I built the end.

Find something the wizard broke. Put it back together. Get the dirt off it. The tower gives you a page of his diary in return.

That loop has to survive being done nine times. It is the reason anybody stays in the tower past the first floor, and it is the reason a game with nothing to lose still has somewhere to go.

First you put it back together

A bust of the wizard is in pieces on the workbench. The repair minigame floats the fragments in front of you and you drag each one home. No timer, no failure, and R puts every piece back where it started, because a cozy game cannot afford a move you regret.

The bust, reassembled a fragment at a time.

Then you get the dirt off it

Cleaning is the interaction the whole game rests on. A shader keeps a mask over the artefact, your cursor is a soft brush that lifts dirt with falloff so it feels organic rather than geometric, and the system rasterizes the mesh UVs first so progress only counts over geometry you can actually see. At 95% it calls it done, and that last five percent is deliberate: nobody should have to hunt a single stubborn pixel behind an ear.

The bust again, whole now and still filthy. Watch the end: it turns itself to show you, and the minigame closes without being asked.

And one that is neither

The library has a star globe instead. You turn it until the constellations match what the wizard’s notes told you, which is a different verb for the same promise: the game will tell you plainly when you are right, and it will never punish you for being wrong on the way there.

The star globe on the library floor. Q and E, and no clock.

And the one at the top

The beacon at the top is the last of them and the only one that asks something real of you. Three concentric rings, nine seasonal inscriptions, and a rotation the game refuses to hint at beyond the logic of the rings themselves. It has spent four floors quietly teaching you how to read it, and it is the last thing on this page.

Who wrote which part

I built cleaning and repair with another programmer on the team, on calls that ran five hours at a stretch, and came into the beacon the same way near the end. One of us drove and the other navigated, and we swapped whenever the person typing started making the same mistake twice. I would do it that way again on purpose: you get somebody’s reasoning out loud while they are still having it, which is the fastest I have ever learned a codebase, and it left two people carrying the whole model instead of one.

What I own outright is where those minigames end. The artefact turns when it is done. The progress bar. The minigame closing itself instead of sitting there waiting to be dismissed. The handoff into the journal. Cleaning something should feel like finishing, and finishing is a separate design problem from the mechanic.

The payoff is not the mechanic You can build a cleaning system that works perfectly and still leave the player in a closed loop wondering whether anything happened. The last half second is where a minigame becomes satisfying, and it is usually the half second nobody has been assigned.
The Journal

The wizard’s story arrives because you earned it, not because a cutscene decided it was time.

Pages start locked. A page unlocks when you finish a puzzle. That is the whole narrative delivery system.

I built the journal: the locked pages, the overlays, the layout, and the wiring that opens a page when an artefact is finished. The chain runs from the cleanable item through the game state manager into the journal UI, which means the story responds to progress rather than to sequence. Finish a floor’s puzzles in any order you like and the entries still arrive in the right one.

the unlock chain
cleanable.gd           artefact reaches 95%, emits finished
        ↓
game_state_manager.gd  records floor progress, in any order
        ↓
journal_ui.gd          unlocks the next page and opens to it

Three files, one rule: the story responds to progress, never to sequence.

It matters because of what kind of game this is. There is nothing to lose in Equinox, so curiosity has to do the work that fear does everywhere else. The journal is that engine. You clean a thing, you learn a thing, and the reason to climb the next set of stairs is that you want to know what he did next.

The same clean, picked up two seconds later. The journal opens itself to the page you just earned.

Day One

Before any art arrived, I wrote the file that stopped fourteen people corrupting each other’s work.

Godot, 3D models, textures, audio, and a repo on its way to 190 MB. On day one, a .gitattributes.

Binary files do not merge. Two people touching the same model or the same scene file produce a conflict git cannot resolve, and on a jam clock that costs an afternoon nobody has to spare. Git LFS fixes it by keeping the large files outside the repository proper, but only if the tracking rules exist before the assets land.

.gitattributes
# 3D Models
*.fbx  *.gltf  *.glb  *.blend  *.obj

# Images
*.png  *.jpg  *.tga  *.webp  *.exr  *.hdr  *.dds

# Audio
*.mp3  *.wav  *.ogg

# Godot LFS Specific
*.scn  *.res  *.material  *.anim  *.mesh  *.lmbake
    filter=lfs diff=lfs merge=lfs -text

Committed 10 January, the first day of the jam, before a single asset existed.

The last block is the part people miss. Most gamedev templates cover models, images and audio. Godot has its own binary formats on top of those, and .scn, .res, .material, .anim, .mesh and .lmbake will bloat a repository and conflict just as badly if nobody tracks them. It is the least glamorous thing on this page and it is the one I would do again first.

The same file, from the other end On a 140 person project I later ran the retroactive version of this: a full history rewrite over one weekend, on a repo that had not done it on day one, with a recovery in the middle of it. Doing it at the start takes twenty minutes. That story is in Path to Menzoberranzan.
Takeaways

What my first jam taught me that a solo project could not.

01

The payoff is a job

Every moment a game confirms you did something right is a design problem of its own, and on most teams nobody owns it. I did, and it is what the scoreboard turned out to be measuring.

02

Build the floor before anyone needs to stand on it

Somebody built the movement and the model pipeline inside forty eight hours so that a first timer could be useful on day three. That is most of the reason fourteen people shipped anything.

03

The graph records the driver, not the call

Pair programming over Discord is how a lot of jam code gets written, and the commit lands under whoever was typing. Worth knowing when you read anybody’s contribution graph, mine included.

04

The constraint was the brief

“Overcomplicated Screen Transitions” sounds like a gag. It was the only category every entry competed in, and treating it as a pacing problem rather than a visual one is why we placed second in it.

The top of the tower

Solve the beacon and the tower does the rest. The rings come down, the light catches properly for the first time, and the winter breaks over the valley while the credits roll. Eighteen days, fourteen people, and this is the minute all of it was for.

Unedited, all the way through: an inscription going home, the flame catching, the rings coming down, and the first sunrise the valley has seen in a very long time.

Making a game is collaborative in a way making a product is not, quite. Everyone’s contribution is load-bearing in a different direction. The artist builds the world, the composer tells you how to feel about it, the voice actor gives the wizard a voice, and the programmers make it run. I came in knowing almost nothing about any of it and left owning the part where the game says well done.

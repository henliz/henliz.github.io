A young woman arrives on stroke protocol.

The first responder who hears a patient’s story is the one nobody downstream gets to ask.

She’s confused and, as far as anyone knows, healthy. The ER does everything right: a full stroke workup, a CT. It’s clear. Then her dad walks in on his own and mentions she drinks every day. That one sentence changes the picture. It was most likely a withdrawal seizure.

Nobody made a mistake. The physician who told me about her said the system shouldn’t need safeguards to catch what better information would have prevented. Somebody at the scene could have known that sentence. Nobody downstream could ask them.

So we built Nightingale.

A button-operated wearable, and a dashboard that carries the reviewed note from fire to paramedics to the ER.

At first contact the firefighter presses record, says aloud that they’re recording for the medical record, and presses a second button to photograph a pill bottle or the scene. It stores everything on the device and syncs when it finds a network it knows. Speech becomes a draft ePCR, unsure fields get flagged instead of guessed, and the officer leading the call approves it before anyone else sees it. Paramedics have it before they arrive. The ER gets it through a path it already uses, with no new login or system.

My product pitch from our MVP video, with the Nightingale film. Sound on.

The film walks through one call. It starts at the station, goes through my sketches and what’s inside the case, shows where it clips onto a turnout coat, and ends on the Maryhill cardiac arrest drill turning into a draft ePCR. Watch for the moment it catches the cousin and the responder giving two different ages and flags it, instead of picking one. That’s the whole idea in one field.

None of it started with the device, though. It started with the people whose job this is.

I went where the story starts.

16 sessions with 18 people in Woolwich, Cambridge and Guelph, and a survey of 29 paramedics and ER nurses.

Firefighters and officers from two volunteer halls and two career stations, a paramedic, two emergency physicians and three ER nurses. I ran most of it as listening sessions: instead of asking people to describe the process, I asked them to tell me about a call they remembered. A story puts someone back in the moment, and it surfaces the workarounds a procedure question never would.

Everyone let me into their halls, trucks and ERs. I promised to ask before publishing any photo with them in it, so for now it’s trucks, forms, the device, and me.

Fire hears the most, and keeps it on a glove.

Fire is often first, and can be alone with a patient for ten to thirty minutes.

01

The captain collects it

Family, medications, allergies, history, the health card, while also running the scene. “You think of the things that you do every day and amp that up 20 fold.”

02

It lives on a glove

Or a flip booklet, or a department phone. The drug count, the number crews trust least, lives in a head or on a glove.

03

It’s said once

Out loud, in about a minute, while the patient is being moved. 20 of the 25 paramedics we surveyed get it by voice only.

04

Then it’s typed three times

Into three systems that don’t talk to each other, hours later. And fire almost never finds out how the patient did.

Because the system is broken, every hall has built its own way around it, and I started treating each workaround as a requirement somebody had already prototyped for us. Maryhill’s deputy chief photographs plates and licences into his own Google Photos because handwriting fails him (“Is that an 86 or a B6?”). Crews bag pill bottles for the ambulance, and Maryhill sends the helmet. A firefighter-EMT up north texted a med list to the incoming paramedics from her own phone, and was told she wasn’t allowed to.

Read together, they told us what has to travel: photos, medications, the scene itself, and the one fact that can’t be missed. They also told us where the line is. Nobody will use a personal phone on a call (“they could subpoena my phone”), and nobody photographs a patient. So anything we built had to belong to the department, capture only what care needs, and stay under the responder’s control.

The ER side is the same story in different words. The report happens at least twice at the door, the written record arrives by fax hours or days later, and the things physicians said they need most, like scene photos and the pill bottles on the counter, are the things that never travel. An ER nurse and a fire captain both called it a broken telephone, separately.

Why hasn’t anyone fixed it? Because people keep catching it.

What holds this together isn’t the system. It’s people being good in a bad situation.

Firefighters get brushed off at the handoff often enough that some have learned to fight for the one fact that can’t be missed. A captain at Cambridge Station 6 has been told “You’re just firefighters.” A Breslau firefighter had a paramedic push past him mid-report at a crash. So a Maryhill captain writes the allergy down on its own, separate from everything else, so that even if the paramedics cut him short he can still get it in: “Hey, they are allergic to aspirin or something that they may give them immediately.”

That isn’t the system working. That’s one captain deciding, every call, that the system won’t be the reason someone gets the wrong drug. Every time someone like him catches it, the failure stays invisible, and an invisible failure never builds enough pressure to get fixed. Honestly, it’s a bit of a miracle it doesn’t go wrong more often.

Underneath that, the system is split. Fire is municipal and sits under MFIPPA. Paramedics and hospitals sit under PHIPA. No record moves between them, and every agency protects its own liability first. We mapped every cause, loop and source into one systems map.

The systems map, rippling out from the core loss. Open the full interactive map, where every node has its sources.

Fire has tried to fix this before, and its own history says what survives. A community program once had patients keep their medication list in an orange canister in the fridge, and crews were trained to look there first. It died because nobody kept the lists up to date, and a firefighter put the problem plainly: “You could get false information.” Meanwhile Who’s Responding, an app where members just tap to say they’re available for a call, was embraced. A Maryhill firefighter summed it up: “If it makes your job easier, it will be embraced.” Tools that add a step get dropped. Tools that remove one stay, so Nightingale couldn’t ask anyone to do one more thing.

About $209M a year, and it lands on the wrong people.

“Information gets lost” is a feeling, and feelings don’t get budgets.

So I built a Monte Carlo cost model for the Conrad School pitch. Every input, from call volumes to wages to adverse-event rates, is a low, most-likely and high range tagged as measured or as my own assumption, and the model draws from all of them 200,000 times. The most likely answer is about $209M a year across Canada, with 80% of runs between $161M and $269M.

The model also showed me where to look. Almost all of its spread comes from one input nobody has measured: how much of fire’s account never reaches the paramedic. That’s the number the next tests are for.

Nobody asked for a wearable.

They asked for an iPad checklist, a shared form, a printed summary, and “something strapped to my arm and it’s listening to all the questions.”

Four formats, one need: capture what the crew learns once, as it happens, and get it to the next person without more work. A form or an iPad still needs a free hand at the exact moment responders have neither. And paramedics already say out loud what they’re doing as they do it. The data is already being spoken. It just isn’t being captured. So the brief came straight from them.

From cardboard to V3.

I had never built hardware before this. Everything in the build log is something I learned by getting it wrong first.

A Pi Zero 2 W, because it’s a real computer small enough to clip on. It records audio in 15-second pieces, keeps the sharpest frame from a photo burst, fingerprints every file and retries uploads, all at once. Pieces, not one long stream, because it has to keep recording while it uploads and transcribes in real time. If the signal drops mid-call, we know exactly which pieces made it and which didn’t, and one cut-out can never corrupt the whole recording. It runs no AI, so it can stay small, and the transcription happens on the server.

The first microphone failed in my bedroom. A lavalier through a sound card came back garbled at 30 cm in the quietest room I had. If it couldn’t work there it couldn’t work on a scene, so I swapped it for an INMP441, a digital mic that skips the sound card entirely. It hears in every direction, which suits a captain collecting the story from family across a room. The trade-off is that it hears everything, noise included.

Photos, never video. A Cambridge crew strongly preferred photos on a button press over anything recording continuously, because of privacy, storage and the legal side, and an ER nurse told us paramedics never pass photos on at all, it’s all verbal. A photo gives the next person what they actually need: the pill bottle, the vehicle damage, how the scene looked before it changed. Video would capture far more of strangers and their homes than care needs, and Ontario’s health privacy law says to collect only what’s reasonably necessary. The camera has autofocus because the person wearing it is moving, and every press takes a quick burst and keeps only the sharpest frame.

Two physical buttons, one job each. Gloves can’t work a touchscreen, so there isn’t one. “If it’s not easy to turn on, it’s not going to get used,” a volunteer firefighter told us, so one press always means one thing, with no holding for three seconds. And it never records or takes a photo on its own, because, as an emergency physician put it, “you need to make sure that the people using it feel safe.”

Nothing is deleted until the server confirms a perfect copy. Scenes happen on highways and farms, where nobody hands over a Wi-Fi password.

Breadboard jumper wires work loose, and a gear pocket isn’t a desk, so everything got soldered. The buttons are tiny and there were no helping hands, so pliers held it all still.

The case took three versions before anything clipped on. We started with cardboard and tape so responders had something to hold in drills straight away, and so we could see where their hands went.

Zubi modelled and printed V2. Every part got laid out flat and measured first, then modelled as a tray and a lid. It came out a lot smaller than the cardboard. Then the camera ribbon fought it. Those ribbons are fickle: a harsh bend or a kink and the camera just stops working, and the print didn’t leave it room to curve. So I sliced the print up, gave the ribbon a path, and put it back together.

V3 had to be half the width and actually fit. We modelled every part one to one, added grooves so each piece has a place, and rounded the edges so it wouldn’t catch on gear. The controls changed too. Capture moved to one big pressure plate in the lid, something you can find and press with a glove on without looking, and power became a small recessed button in the bottom wall, so a bump against a door frame can’t switch it off mid-call.

Where it lives depends on the call. Calls vary so much that there’s no single outfit to design for. Some medical calls are station wear and gloves. If there are hazards, or a lot of blood, it’s bunker pants so they can kneel. If the scene is unsafe, or there’s a fire, it’s full turnout gear. The device has to sit on all three without getting in anyone’s way, and work with both kinds of breathing apparatus pack crews wear. So we sketched where it could go on each one.

A belt clip works on all three. The real decision was how to attach it. One option was a holder on the back of the case that the clip slides into. We ruled it out: anything that slides in can slide out, and a device that drops off mid-call is worse than no device at all. The other was fixing the clip on, with screws or pegs, so it can’t come loose. That’s the one we chose.

So the clip went on with pegs, through a backing printed onto the case. The pressure plate got a lip so it can’t fall through the lid, and every part was modelled where it actually sits.

Printed, it was half the size of V2. The clip fit and held on well. The pegs that hold it mean the back isn’t flat anymore, and the finish still needs cleaning up, so that’s next, along with more feedback from responders wearing it.

A record only helps if the next person trusts it.

I coded dashboard V1, Alita and Chrisandra designed V2’s flows in Figma, and I wired V3 up to the real device data.

V1 tested the technology. Before anything was designed, I needed to know whether the device’s recordings could become a usable draft report while a call was still happening. So I coded a dashboard with three panels: the draft ePCR filling itself in, with fields lighting up as they change; the transcript, showing who said what and when; and the Pi’s own diary, live. It updates the moment new data lands, with no refresh, and every session is saved so it survives a server restart.

V1 was built for our team, not for responders. The interviews, the survey, our user segments and stories, and the flows we mapped from the 911 call all the way to the hospital chart turned it into two connected flows: one for the officer leading the call, who reviews the crew’s account (the captain or chief on the truck, or on a volunteer call, where you don’t know who’s showing up, whoever’s most senior or has the most medical training), and one for the paramedic, who needs to see where each value came from and confirm or correct it. V2 designed that whole handoff in Figma.

  • Review before sharing. A physician told me his colleagues accepted their AI scribe once the reviewed note, not the recording, became the record. Nightingale works the same way.
  • Flagged, never guessed. If something wasn’t said, the field stays visibly empty instead of being filled with a likely answer.
  • A closed loop. The paramedic accepts the record and fire sees that it landed. Nobody in this chain could ever confirm what was received.

No hospital integration, on purpose. The three big hospital record systems, Cerner, Epic and Meditech, all nominally use the same data standards and each modifies them in ways that block real interoperability, then charges heavily for outside connections. That’s a business decision, not a technical limit, and for a student team it’s a dead end. Meanwhile, emergency documentation is still mostly paper, and the paramedic’s report arrives by fax and gets scanned into the chart by clerks hours or days later. So every ER already has a free way to get a page into its system. Exactly how Nightingale rides that path is still open. It’s what our walkthroughs with emergency physicians are for, once we have more first-responder test data to show them a range of calls.

Our first mockup organised the record like the paper form. Five tabs, Summary, Patient, Clinical, Fire sheet and Service, each one its own page. That’s the form’s hierarchy, not the people’s. A captain mid-call, a paramedic on the way and a nurse at triage each need a different few things first, and the tabs made every one of them go looking. It also kept the whole transcript on screen the entire time, right beside the form it was filling in, and responders weren’t fans: a full transcript sitting open is a privacy problem.

So we went form first, and dropped the traditional nav. The user testing and cognitive walkthroughs showed people hunting between tabs for the one answer they needed, so we redesigned around the information instead of the pages. Safety and the most important information come first, ordered by who’s reading: the captain sees what still needs a person, the paramedic sees what changed, the vitals and the hazards. The transcript moved behind the form. It’s there to check a value against, trust but verify, not to read through. Everything you do during a call happens on one screen, in pop-ups built for quick edits. The only other page is the full ePCR, for one last review before it goes to the doctors.

V3 made it work. We rebuilt Alita and Chrisandra’s Figma flows as working code, so responders can click through them, and I wired it to the real drill data coming off the device so we could see how actual scene data fits the design. Here’s Maryhill case 1, the backyard barbecue cardiac arrest, as the dashboard holds it now. Every value below came from the recording, and every clip is the real dashboard running.

Photos come in the same way, from the second button.

Anything the draft isn’t sure about gets flagged, and a flag always explains itself. Walking through the flows, the same three jobs kept coming up: see what’s missing, look into a conflict, and get back to a field that needs fixing. So every flag opens straight onto the speech it came from.

Back on the home screen, the record says what still needs a person before it goes anywhere.

Every other part of the call is a pop-up away, so nobody loses their place mid-call.

The full ePCR is the one extra page, for the final pass before it goes to the doctors.

Its handoff tab tells the whole call as a timeline and a narrative, for whoever reads it next.

On the paramedic’s side, the record is already waiting for whoever’s attending. Paramedics work in pairs, one driving and one attending, and they swap after every call to give their brains a break, so the record goes to the role, not a fixed person. It leads with what changed, the vitals and the scene hazards, short enough to scan on the way. That came from the survey: 7 of 25 paramedics said they’d ask the patient everything again themselves anyway, so a longer report wasn’t the answer. A head start on their own assessment was.

Before the ER, they verify it, and anything nobody said is called out instead of guessed.

Privacy was the reason anyone would use it.

Responders and physicians raised privacy and control more than anything else, so Cindy and I made them the first thing the backend got right.

Cindy and I worked out the security and privacy approach and built it together, with Cindy taking the lead on more of the implementation. Here’s what it does now:

  • Nothing opens without a key, and a key only opens what it’s allowed to. The production server won’t even start without them.
  • A record and its photos belong to whoever made them. Reading someone else’s takes an explicit admin override, and that gets logged too.
  • Every read and write leaves a trail that can’t be quietly edited. Each audit entry is chained to the one before it, so changing an old entry breaks the chain.
  • The device only talks over HTTPS, with its own token. Uploads are encrypted at rest, and files on the Pi and the server are locked down.
  • The Pi’s diary records what happened, never what was said. “Photo saved” and “Wi-Fi lost,” but no audio, transcript or images.
It isn’t PHIPA-ready, and we wrote that downOur threat model puts every remaining gap next to the control we have: no real identity provider or MFA yet, no case-based sharing policy, token rotation unfinished. We only use fictional drill data, and our readiness checklist says plainly that it’s not a certification or approval to use real patient information.

The night the Wi-Fi died.

I can’t record real patients, so I tested on staged calls with the people who’d really use it.

Three calls on Maryhill’s training nights, under a floodlight on foam mats: a cardiac arrest at a backyard barbecue, a shellfish allergy, and a dirt bike crash. During the first one, the station Wi-Fi died. Offline-first had only ever been a hypothetical, and I’ll admit I dreaded opening the results that night. All three calls had arrived, every file stored, checked and in order.

The draft caught the chief complaint, interventions and clinical details with nobody typing them, and flagged an age a bystander and a responder had given differently. At the end of the dirt bike call, a paramedic joked about the leg and everyone laughed. That joke isn’t in the output.

It also showed me what’s not working yet: a 30-second boot no crew will wait for, nothing reaching the server live, and, at the time, no clip on the case. A captain called it “a step in the right direction” that could be “very easily adopted,” on one condition: security has to hit the mark.

What it doesn’t prove yet.

Staged calls are a first signal, not a result.

  • Cambridge Fire’s probationary firefighters on their own training scenarios, the first test where the script isn’t ours. If capture slows the call or crews throw most of the draft away, the design fails and we start again.
  • Guelph paramedics, then their station.
  • The fixes: a standby mode for the boot time and a hotspot for live sending.
  • The clip and the case: the clip’s stability and the case’s integrity on real gear, with both SCBA pack types, now that the case has roughly its final shape.
  • The legal path for a fire record to reach paramedics under MFIPPA and PHIPA.
  • The number nobody has: how much of fire’s account actually reaches the paramedic.

What I’m taking from it.

Four things I’d carry into anything that has to be trusted.

01

Read the need, not the solution

Three people asked for an iPad, a form and a listening device. They were all asking for the same thing, and if I’d built any one of them as described, I’d have built the wrong tool.

02

Remove a step or don’t bother

Fire’s own history is clear about this. Anything that adds work on scene gets dropped, however good it is.

03

Trust is the design

Review before sharing, flagged fields instead of guesses, photos instead of video, a closed loop instead of hope. Every one of those makes the tool claim less, and every one came from someone telling me what would make them refuse to use it.

04

Test the failure before you need it

The offline-first path was a hypothetical until the Wi-Fi died. It held because I’d built for the network failing, not for it working.

The story is never lost at the scene. It’s lost on the way out, and everyone downstream pays for it.

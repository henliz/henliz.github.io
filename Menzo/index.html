The Project

Menzoberranzan has never existed in a Baldur's Gate game. We're fixing that.

Path to Menzoberranzan is an unofficial expansion for Baldur's Gate 3 — a full custom campaign set deep in the Underdark, in the city the franchise was always too cautious to fully render. The project spans original narrative, original art, original audio, original cinematics, and original engineering. It is not a mod in the sense of a texture swap or a balance patch. It is a production, with departments, pipelines, documentation, and the kind of technical debt that can silently crater a year's worth of work if you're not paying attention.

It's built entirely by volunteers who don't have to care — but do. That's the strangest and most sustaining thing about it. People are here, working late on this, because Menzoberranzan deserves to exist, it's a city the series never fully explored.

The Leadership Structure

CTO and Technical Director. Same person. Completely different jobs.

This distinction matters and is usually collapsed into one vague "technical lead" title until something catastrophic happens. PtM formalised it early because we hit the scale where leaving it implicit was actively dangerous. At 140+ contributors, a single ambiguous decision can propagate silently through the pipeline for weeks before it surfaces as months of wasted work.

"CTO failures are long-range and invisible. Technical Director failures are immediate and production-killing. They are not the same job wearing different hats — they are categorically different engineering problems." — Hen's Brain: Technical Leadership Manual

CTO — Chief Technical Officer

"Prevents future collapse."

  • Long-term system stability and architecture
  • Repository structure, LFS rules, versioning conventions
  • Custom toolchain design and governance
  • Licensing strategy (PolyForm Shield 1.0.0)
  • Cross-department dependency mapping
  • Risk prevention before hazards form
  • Academic constraints (CHI submission considerations)

Technical Director

"Prevents present paralysis."

  • In-engine implementation — BG3 Toolkit daily
  • Debugging undocumented engine anomalies in real time
  • Unblocking cinematics, quests, audio, and environments
  • TL/CL inheritance, Osiris story logic, atmosphere systems
  • Translating architecture into practical in-engine workflows
  • Rapid diagnosis under production pressure

The CTO asks: will this decision hurt us in six months? The Technical Director asks: why is this cinematic broken right now and how do I fix it before the department goes idle? Neither question can wait for the other. They run in parallel, constantly, across different timescales and failure modes. Merging them into one "technical lead" role doesn't simplify the work — it just means both fail quietly until they fail loudly.

The Problem Space

140 people. Zero salary. An undocumented engine. A 21-year-old in charge of the infrastructure.

PtM's technical complexity arises from a stack of compounding constraints that don't exist in funded studios: no documentation from the engine developer, no on-call support, contributors in a dozen time zones with variable availability, and pipelines built entirely from scratch by reverse-engineering a closed system. The Baldur's Gate 3 Toolkit exposes a fraction of what the engine actually does. The rest is detective work — pattern recognition, edge cases, anomalies that behave one way on Tuesday and differently on Friday for reasons that take hours to isolate.

There is no margin for architectural error here. In a paid studio, bad infrastructure costs money and time. In a volunteer studio, it costs people — contributors who burn out, quit, or lose faith in the project because their work keeps breaking for reasons they don't control. Technical stability isn't a feature in an environment like this. It's the thing that keeps the community together.

The asymmetry of volunteer production

In a paid studio, you can compel a contributor to fix a broken pipeline. In a volunteer studio, if the process is bad enough, they simply leave. Good infrastructure isn't optional — it's what makes people want to stay.

The documentation problem

Larian publishes a toolkit. They do not publish how it works. Lighting inheritance, atmosphere resets, Osiris story node behaviour, TL actor transforms — all of it is learned by breaking things until you understand what you broke.

The single-point-of-failure problem

At PtM's scale, institutional knowledge living in one person's head is not an inconvenience — it's an existential risk. The brain doc exists specifically to externalise the spine of the project so that it survives illness, burnout, academics, and time zones.

The cost of invisible failures

CTO-level mistakes don't announce themselves. They accumulate silently for weeks — incorrect LFS tracking, unsound pipeline assumptions, misaligned versioning — until they surface as corrupted assets or irreversible repository issues. By then, recovery is brutal.

Infrastructure Decisions

The repo was 2.5 GB. Without intervention, it was heading for 80.

Baldur's Gate 3 modding is a binary-heavy discipline. 3D models, textures, audio files, cinematic timelines — none of it is text, none of it diffs cleanly, and all of it grows without mercy. A repository that clones in three minutes becomes one that clones in three hours, and then doesn't clone at all. That's not a developer experience problem. That's a contributor retention problem.

Why Git LFS, and why self-hosted?

Git LFS (Large File Storage) solves the binary bloat problem by keeping large files external to the repository proper — contributors get pointer files in their clone, and the actual binaries are fetched on demand. The repo stays lightweight. The workflow stays sane.

The migration plan I wrote covered every step: audit (find all files in history larger than 10MB), provision new infrastructure on Hetzner, install Forgejo (our self-hosted Git platform), migrate existing LFS objects to Backblaze B2 for external storage, freeze all open PRs, rewrite history, verify, communicate, and archive the old instance. Fourteen steps, a rollback plan, a troubleshooting guide, and a FAQ written specifically for fellow members of the board of directors. It was not a quick decision. It was a surgery plan.

Git over GitHub — self-hosted on Hetzner

GitHub's LFS bandwidth limits would have cost real money at the binary scale PtM operates. We self-host Forgejo on Hetzner instead — significantly cheaper than DigitalOcean or AWS for EU-based teams, with generous bandwidth, fast NVMe storage, and no per-seat licensing surprises. Full control over LFS policies, storage backends (Backblaze B2), and contributor access. The cost difference alone justified the migration.

Why not Perforce?

Perforce is what AAA studios use for binary-heavy version control. It's also designed for teams with server engineers, IT departments, and licensing budgets. For a volunteer studio where "ops" is one person between lectures, Perforce would have been a beautiful, expensive system that nobody could maintain. Git + LFS + Forgejo gives us 80% of Perforce's binary handling at a fraction of the operational complexity.

What the Work Actually Looks Like

It's mostly writing. And thinking. And preventing disasters nobody sees.

The visible part of technical leadership is debugging — the dramatic moment where the cinematic is broken and you find the one misaligned atmosphere preset that caused it. That part is real and takes real skill. But the invisible part is the architecture: the documents, the conventions, the LFS tracking rules, the licensing decisions, the pipeline constraints that mean the department's work doesn't silently invalidate each other over a month.

The brain doc — Hen's Brain: Technical Leadership Manual — exists because the technical spine of this project lived in one person's working memory, and that is a single point of failure the project cannot afford. It defines the two domains, their failure modes, their escalation paths, and the decisions that fall unambiguously into each. It is not theory. It is operational infrastructure, written down.

"Technical debt is not a minor inconvenience in a project at this scale — it is fatal. Uncoordinated use of Script Extender, incorrect patching, inconsistent LFS tracking, or unsound pipeline assumptions can invalidate months of work across entire departments." — Hen's Brain: Technical Leadership Manual

What I can show you is the thinking — the documentation, the governance structure, the infrastructure decisions, and the approach to running a production at this scale without losing contributors to avoidable friction. The actual technical implementations stay inside the NDA. What you're reading is the public surface of a much deeper thing.

Licensing: PtM's custom toolchains are protected under PolyForm Shield 1.0.0 — preventing commercial appropriation of work built by volunteers, for free, for a community. This is a legal architecture decision, not just a creative one.
Being 21 doing this

There is no playbook for running a 140+ person volunteer studio at 21 while completing a degree. The brain doc is partly a technical manual and partly the documentation of hard-won lessons about what happens when ambiguity meets scale. Mostly: things break slowly, and then all at once.

The asymmetric stakes

Nobody is getting paid. Every contributor is here by choice. That means the cost of a bad infrastructure decision isn't just technical debt — it's someone's evening, their weekend, their goodwill. The people working on this trusted us to make it worth their time. That weight is real.

Takeaways

What this project taught me that a classroom couldn't.

01

Name the domains or watch them collapse

CTO and Technical Director are not titles. They're categorical separations that prevent one person from failing at two completely different jobs simultaneously.

02

Infrastructure is community

In a volunteer studio, bad tooling doesn't just slow people down — it drives them away. The repository is the environment everyone lives in. Design it accordingly.

03

Document the spine before it breaks

Institutional knowledge in one person's head is a single point of failure. Externalise it before something goes wrong, not after.

04

Right tool, not best tool

Perforce is excellent. It is also overkill for a team with no ops staff and no budget. Git + LFS + Forgejo solves the actual problem we have, not the theoretical one.

What I can't say yet — and why that's okay

There is an entire category of work on this project that I'm proud of and not allowed to discuss — the actual technical architecture, the custom systems, the in-engine implementations that took months to figure out. The public face of PtM is screenshots and progress updates. The private face is engineering decisions that I can't share because the project isn't finished and the work belongs to the whole team, not just me.

That's the right call. And it's a strange thing, to have done work you can't show. But the discipline of keeping quiet about active development — of understanding that the story gets told when it's done, not while it's happening — is itself a leadership skill. You get to see the infrastructure decisions. You don't get to see the blueprints. Some things are worth waiting for.

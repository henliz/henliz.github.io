SKIP → THE TAKEAWAYS

It is three in the morning and the phone is at four percent.

Every product built for new parents assumes a spare hand and a spare mind. At 3 a.m. there is neither.

Postpartum depression and anxiety reach roughly one in seven mothers. About half of those cases are never detected, because the people living inside them are too tired, too ashamed, or too busy being told they should feel lucky. The window is also longer than anyone plans for. Around 7.2% are still symptomatic nine to ten months out, and more than half of those had no symptoms at all earlier on.

So the need was never the question. The delivery was. Every serious attempt at this problem is an app, and an app asks a parent to download something, invent a password, answer twenty questions about her mood, and then come back daily to log how she is doing. That is homework. Handing homework to somebody running on four broken hours of sleep is not support. It is one more thing to fail at.

The reframeDo not build another mental health app. Build the thing a frightened parent already has open at 3 a.m., which is a phone.

That idea took about nine minutes. The other thirty five hours and fifty one minutes went on finding out what it costs.

We asked five mothers and then shut up.

No surveys. A survey tells you what somebody is willing to put in a form.

We ran Indi Young style listening sessions, which is a polite way of saying we asked one question and then got out of the way for an hour. Five mothers: a first timer with an infant, a mother of twins, one juggling a toddler and a newborn, one still expecting, and one whose children are grown and who had the clearest memory of the whole thing.

The sentence that built the product arrived in the second session. She said she did not want to be onboarded. She wanted to be held together.

“When I’m exhausted, even opening an app feels like a test.”

Mother of one toddler and one infant

“I need someone to tell me what’s normal, and what’s not.”

First time mother with an infant

“If it’s serious, I want the handoff to be gentle, not scary.”

Expectant mother, due soon

“Please remember I had a C-section so I don’t have to retell it.”

First time mother of twins

“I was on a help line for hours and they did their best but weren’t really for that kind of thing.”

Mother of three, children older

A judge picked up the phone and she answered.

Skip to the end of the weekend for a second, because everything after this makes more sense once you have heard her.

The demo was not a video. It was a phone number on a slide, and we asked the room to dial it.

She answered in under five seconds. Comforted first, then two local lactation lines, then a follow up text so the guidance outlived the panic. Behind her on the screen we left the receipts running: the classifier's output, the row landing in the database, the query pulling it back out. Here is the thing itself, before any of the explaining.

The voice line. Same persona sized for audio, and it texts you afterwards so nothing is lost.

Thirty six hours earlier none of it existed. The rest of this is how a weekend got from there to here, and what we refused to build along the way.

The channel was the product.

Distribution is not a marketing problem you get to solve after the build. Here it was the build.

SMS shipped first because SMS is the most boring and most reliable thing in the world. It works at four percent battery, on a cracked screen, in a hospital corridor with one bar, and it never needs updating. Voice came second, once texting was steady, because a parent holding a baby has no hands.

The text line. One caring line, one action, one thing to watch, with region aware resources attached.

Same Auntie on both. That sounds obvious and it is the part most multi channel products get wrong: they let the voice assistant become a brighter, more corporate person than the chat. We wrote one persona and sized her per channel. Texts get one breath. Calls get warmth, real pauses, and permission to trail off. After any call she texts you the key steps, because nobody at 3 a.m. remembers what a kind voice just said.

One breath, one step, one thing to watch.

Our first drafts were paragraphs. A tired parent skims a paragraph and misses the only sentence that mattered.

So we made a rule, and then we made the rule testable. If a message took two breaths to read out loud, it got cut. Reading them aloud was not a flourish, it was the test, and it killed more copy than anything else we did.

01

Say the kind thing first

You are not alone. That line buys the next twelve seconds of her attention, and nothing else works without it.

02

Give exactly one action

Not three options with trade-offs. One. Skin to skin. Call this number. Go now.

03

Name what to watch

One specific thing that would change the answer, so she knows when to stop waiting and act.

04

Then read it out loud

Two breaths meant too long. This is the only rule that reliably caught our own cleverness.

The escalation rewriteOur first red flag script said seek medical attention. Clinically correct, emotionally useless. It became: Love, with a baby this young that can be serious. Let’s head to the nearest emergency department now, I’m sending the address. You’re not alone. The mothers we had interviewed confirmed it read as safe without reading as frightening.

The voice had the same problem in a different key. OpenAI’s realtime text to speech is warm out of the box and also, in certain voices, deeply uncanny. We auditioned until Auntie sounded like somebody’s actual aunt rather than a hold message.

The one part that was never allowed to improvise.

A warm companion that guesses about a newborn’s fever is not a warm companion. It is a liability with a nice voice.

So the model never answers. It classifies. Gemini reads the message and returns a structure: an intent, a topic, a region, a draft reply, and any red flags it spotted. The intent can only ever be one of three things.

ComfortResourceEscalate

From there it is deterministic code. Topic plus region goes to Snowflake and comes back with two or three vetted resources you can actually phone. The evidence is short pre written cards drawn from the WHO, the CDC, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and Postpartum Support International, fed in rather than recalled. Auntie never freestyles medical advice because she structurally cannot.

Contracts are what let four people ship like one mindThe classifier service returns { intent, topic, region, reply_text, red_flags }. The phone and text service asks the resource database for the local lines and the profile store for the small bit of context. Nobody had to read anybody else’s code, and nothing had to be rewritten at hour thirty.

Four services, four owners, one written contract between each pair.

Memory is the other thing we kept deliberately small. Four fields: delivery type, feeding, weeks postpartum, region, tied to a hashed number and short lived. Enough that a mother who had a caesarean never has to say so twice. Not enough to feel watched.

Evidence as pre written cards, so the answer is retrieved and never invented.

What we cut to get there: WhatsApp, every dashboard, and semantic embeddings over the whole corpus. A short list of topic tags and a small lookup answered faster and were far easier to reason about at hour thirty with four underslept people.

What I would carry into the next one.

A hackathon is a cheap way to find out which of your instincts are real.

01

Ship the channel, not the chore

Phone and SMS beat any elegant onboarding when attention is the scarce resource. Distribution was the design decision, not a launch task.

02

Warmth is a feature with a spec

One breath, one step, one thing to watch. Written down it stops being a vibe and starts being something a draft can fail.

03

Safety is a contract, not a disclaimer

Structured intents and deterministic lookups mean the model cannot improvise the dangerous part. A design choice long before an engineering one.

04

Personalise light, and say so

Four fields, hashed, short lived. Remembering the caesarean matters. Remembering everything is surveillance with a friendly voice.

The thing I did not expect was how much of it was writing. Not copywriting, writing: the taxonomy, the red flag lexicon, the escalation script, the four fields we were willing to keep. By Sunday the repository held less code than I assumed it would and more decisions than I thought a weekend could hold.

And the instinct that turned out to be real is the one I keep building on. The load was always going to exist. The only question is whether the system carries it or the person does.
